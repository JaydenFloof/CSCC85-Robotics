Q1) What is the total number of probabilities your robot has to keep 
    track of for a map of size m x n?

The total number of probabilities is 4 * m * n, as there are 4 possible
directions the robot can take on a map of size m x n.


Q2) What should be the initial value of the probabilities your robot is 
    keeping track of?

The initial value of the probabilities should be (1 / [4 * m * n]), as 
all possible directions and locations are equally likely and must sum up
to 1.


Q3) Describe your strategy for dealing with sensor noise, and
    explain why it should work and how you tested it.

Multiple color readings are taken and averaged to produce the most likely
color. We assume the color sensor noise is Gaussian, and we obtain two 
useful properties:

- The amount of noise for each measurement is independent of other sensor readings.
- Error values close to zero are more likely.
- Error values away from zero become less likely the farther they are.

This was tested empirically via the level of information consistency
provided by sensor calibration.


Q4) Describe your probability update function in detail and
    explain how you defined 'agreement' as well as how you
    Determined the amount by which to update probabilities.

The probability update function is a 3D matrix, where each cell 
corresponds to a potential location and direction of the robot. 
For each update iteration, we take color sensor readings of each 
corner of the current intersection and compute the probability matrix
(i.e., the 'agreement') as follows:

- N = # of matching color readings.
- M = # of non-matching color readings.
- P(correct_color_reading) = 0.85
- P = P(correct_color_reading)^N + (1 - P(correct_color_reading))^M

At the end of the iteration, all probabilities are normalized.


Q5) Describe in detail your robot’s exploration strategy – it should
    be more complex than simply moving in the same direction
    until hitting the boundary of the map. How did you choose this
    Strategy?

The robot's exploration strategy is as follows:

- Choose a random direction, except backwards. (Random Sampling)
- Scan the corners of the intersection. (Gather belief data)
- Repeat until confidence in localization is sufficient.

This strategy was chosen for simplicity and fairness to all possible
locations. The exploration strategy does not prevent revisiting 
intersections, although this is largely not a problem, as it may 
correct previous erroneous sensor readings at such intersections.


Q6) Describe in detail how beliefs are shifted when the robot gets
    to a boundary. Consider this problem carefully, the correct
    way to handle this is not obvious. Explain clearly what
    assumptions regarding the robot's motion support your
    strategy for shifting belief values in this case.

When the robot gets to a boundary, it returns to the previous intersection
and makes a right turn. When a new intersection is eventually found, the 
action model is updated to consider the actions which led to reaching
this new intersection.

As a result, hitting the boundary does not update our beliefs whatsoever.
New localization information *could* be gleamed from hitting the boundary, 
but reading intersections is not only typically more useful than boundary 
information, it also does not "pollute" the beliefs with data that does not
follow the designed localization format. (i.e., intersection corner readings)


Q7) Describe in detail how beliefs are shifted when the robot turns
    90 degrees (either left or right) without going to a different
    Intersection. Describe your assumptions about robot motion,
    and how these support your strategy for handling this case.

Beliefs are not shifted when the robot merely turns 90 degrees. 
Instead, beliefs are shifted when the robot turns AND moves forward. 

We assume the robot's probability to turn and move forward along an 
intersection is 50%, turning diagonally 20% (both diagonals), and not 
turning enough 10%, as per the lecture notes.


Q8) Explain your method for deciding when the robot has achieved
    correct localization.

The belief confidence threshold to end localization and proceed to the 
location defined by (dest_x, dest_y) is 90%. Otherwise, the robot will 
resume localization.


Q9) Briefly explain your robot design choices, including sensor placement
    and robot mobility considerations.

The color sensor is placed directly in the center, below the robot to 
prevent ambient light from affecting readings. For intersection turns, 
we found that moving the robot backwards by half of the distance its 
wheels are apart (constant), turning to another intersection becomes 
surprisingly consistent! 

As such, the wheels were designed to maximize grip, and thus minimize
the turning error.


Q10) What parameters did you find affect the most the ability of the robot to
     determine its location?

The gaps in the paper of the physical map often caused the NXT color sensor
to read the incorrect colors, and lead the robot astray despite being in the
correct location. (Often an intersection.)

Furthermore, the color and gyro sensor would often fail (values keep climbing 
without end), causing the robot to continuously perform a single action, such as 
street realignment. At times, even the strength of the bluetooth connection and 
battery capacity of the EV3 would determine either the responsiveness and 
movement consistency of the robot, respectively.


Q11) Can external factors (illumination in the room, for example) affect the
     ability of the robot to find itself?

No, the color sensor is placed directly underneath the center of the robot 
to avoid issues with illumination. 

Aside: Previous designs used two "hoods" above the sensor to cover any 
external light where the color sensor reads.


Q12) On average, how many intersections does your robot need to visit
     to achieve successful localization?

Three intersections need to be visited on average before the robot 
achieves successful localization.


Q13) Are there locations in the map that make localization harder? (i.e.
     does it matter where your robot starts its journey? Why?)

Starting on an intersection with non-unique corner configurations 
(i.e., some intersections have the same colors, with possibly differing
orientations) often delay localization, as the robot can be less sure of 
which intersection it is travelling on.

Since we chose to randomly select a direction to go for localization, 
starting near the corners of the grid map may cause the robot to hit a 
boundary and thus require more turns to properly begin localization.
